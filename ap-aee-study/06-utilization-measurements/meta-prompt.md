### 2.1 ELECTRIC TRACTION [M — at least 1 Q]

**Tractive effort (F) [HV]:**
$$F = \frac{\eta \cdot P \cdot 1000}{v} \text{ N (P in kW, v in m/s)}$$
$$F = \frac{375 \cdot \eta \cdot P_{hp}}{V_{km/h}} \text{ N}$$

- $P$ = power at wheels (kW) or at motor shaft (different formulas)
- $v$ = velocity (m/s or km/h)
- $\eta$ = efficiency of transmission + motor
- $F$ = tractive effort in Newtons

**Real-life anchor:** Indian Railways uses 25 kV AC traction with 3-phase induction motors. The locomotive's tractive effort must overcome gravity (gradients), friction, and air drag.

**Speed-time curves (trapezoidal, simplified):**
- **Period 1:** Acceleration (constant $a_1$, $v$ rises linearly)
- **Period 2:** Constant speed (free-running, $v = v_{max}$)
- **Period 3:** Coasting (motoring off, $a_2$ = deceleration due to friction)
- **Period 4:** Braking ($a_3$ = strong deceleration)

**Definitions [M]:**
- **Crest speed ($V_{max}$):** Maximum speed reached
- **Average speed ($V_{avg}$):** Distance / total time (including stops)
- **Schedule speed ($V_{sch}$):** Distance / (running time + station stop time)
- $V_{avg} < V_{sch} < V_{crest}$

**Specific Energy Consumption (SEC):**
$$SEC = \frac{\text{Energy consumed (Wh)}}{\text{Weight (tonnes)} \times \text{Distance (km)}}$$
Lower is better. Modern electric traction: 50-80 Wh/tonne-km. Diesel: 250-400 Wh/tonne-km.

**Adhesion and wheel slip:**
- Tractive effort ≤ Co-efficient of adhesion × Weight on driven wheels
- $\mu$ typically 0.25-0.35 for steel wheel on steel rail
- Slip occurs when F > $\mu W$

**Traction motor types:**
- DC series motor: traditional, good torque
- 3-φ induction motor: modern (more reliable, lower maintenance)
- Synchronous motor: very high power (some electric locomotives)

**Indian context:**
- 25 kV AC, 50 Hz overhead catenary (most of IR network)
- DC traction (1500V or 3000V DC) — older sections, being phased out
- 3-φ locomotives like WAP-5, WAP-7 use 3-φ induction motors with VFD
- Mumbai suburban uses DC (1500V); Delhi metro uses 25kV AC

**Traction calculations often combine with motors:**
- "Find tractive effort of a 4000 hp locomotive at 80 km/h"
- Power at wheels (assume η = 0.85)
- $F = 375 \times 0.85 \times 4000 / 80 = 15,938$ N

### 2.2 ELECTRIC HEATING [M]

**Resistance heating:**
$$P = V^2/R = I^2 R \quad \text{(Joule heating)}$$
- Direct heating: current passes through material to be heated
- Indirect heating: heating element in furnace; material in separate chamber
- Used in: ovens, furnaces, water heaters, space heaters

**Induction heating [M]:**
- AC current in coil creates alternating magnetic field
- Field induces eddy currents in the workpiece (conductor)
- Eddy current losses = heating
- Power density: $P = k \cdot B^2_{max} \cdot f^2 \cdot d^2 \cdot \rho$ (rough)
- **Depth of penetration:** $\delta = \sqrt{\frac{2\rho}{\omega \mu}}$
  - Higher frequency → shallower heating (surface)
  - Lower frequency → deeper heating
  - Used for: surface hardening (high f), through-heating (low f)
- **Frequencies:** 50 Hz (mains) for bulk heating; 1-100 kHz (MF) for surface; 100 kHz-1 MHz (RF) for special

**Dielectric heating:**
- For non-conductors (wood, plastic, rubber, food)
- AC field at high frequency (10-100 MHz)
- Polarization losses in dielectric
- Power: $P = V^2 \omega C \tan\delta$
- Used for: plywood gluing, plastic welding, food processing

**Arc heating:**
- Electric arc between electrodes and workpiece
- Very high temperatures (3000-4000°C)
- Used in: arc furnaces (steel making), arc welding

**Comparison of heating methods:**
| Method | Temp range | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Resistance | Up to 1000°C | Ovens, water heaters |
| Induction | 1000-3000°C | Melting, surface hardening |
| Arc | 3000-4000°C | Steel making |
| Dielectric | 50-200°C | Plastics, wood |

### 2.3 ELECTRIC WELDING [M]

**Resistance welding:**
- Heat generated by $I^2 R t$ at the joint
- $I$ = 1000-100,000 A; $R$ = contact resistance; $t$ = 0.01-1 sec

**Types:**
- **Spot welding:** Two electrodes press sheets together, current flows through, fuses
- **Seam welding:** Continuous spot welding with roller electrodes (for tanks, containers)
- **Butt welding:** Two parts pressed end-to-end, current through joint
- **Projection welding:** Similar to spot, but raised projections concentrate current

**Arc welding:**
- Electric arc between electrode and workpiece
- Electrode may be consumable (stick welding) or non-consumable (TIG)
- Shielding gas (Ar, CO₂) protects weld pool

**Comparison:**
- Resistance welding: thin sheets, mass production
- Arc welding: heavy structures, shipbuilding, pipelines
- TIG (Tungsten Inert Gas): high-quality welds, stainless steel
- MIG (Metal Inert Gas): faster, mild steel

**Real-life anchor:** Every car body has ~5000 spot welds. L&T, BHEL, and Indian Shipyards use arc welding heavily.

### 2.4 ILLUMINATION [L]

**Laws of illumination:**
- **Inverse square law:** $E \propto 1/d^2$ (E at distance d from point source)
- **Cosine law (Lambert's):** $E \propto \cos\theta$ (θ = angle of incidence)

**Luminous quantities:**
- Luminous flux (Φ): lumens (lm)
- Luminous intensity (I): candela (cd) = lm/sr
- Illuminance (E): lux = lm/m²
- Luminance (L): cd/m²
- Luminous efficacy: lm/W (efficacy of light source)

**Common values:**
- Incandescent: 10-15 lm/W
- Fluorescent: 50-80 lm/W
- LED: 100-150 lm/W
- Sodium vapor: 100-150 lm/W

### 2.5 BRIDGES FOR MEASUREMENT [M]

**Wheatstone Bridge (Resistance):**
$$R_x = R_2 \cdot R_3/R_1 \quad \text{(balanced condition: galvanometer reads 0)}$$
- For medium resistance (1Ω to 1MΩ)
- Sensitivity depends on bridge arm ratios and galvanometer sensitivity

**Kelvin Double Bridge (Low R < 1Ω):**
- Eliminates lead and contact resistance
- Has additional ratio arms for compensation
- Used in: shunt resistance measurement, cable resistance

**Maxwell Bridge (Inductance, Q < 10):**
$$L_x = R_2 R_3 C_1, \quad R_x = R_2 R_3/R_1$$
- For measuring unknown inductance
- Q is moderate

**Hay Bridge (Inductance, Q > 10):**
- Similar to Maxwell, better for high-Q inductors
- Uses a series resistance with the capacitor

**Schering Bridge (Capacitance, dielectric loss):**
- Measures capacitance and dissipation factor
- Used for insulation testing of cables, transformer oil

**Wien Bridge (Frequency):**
$$f = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{R_1 R_2 C_1 C_2}}$$
- Used in audio frequency measurements, harmonic distortion analyzers

**🚩 EXAMINER TRAP:** Know which bridge for what:
- R (1Ω-1MΩ): Wheatstone
- R (< 1Ω): Kelvin
- L (medium Q): Maxwell
- L (high Q): Hay
- C + dielectric loss: Schering
- Frequency: Wien

### 2.6 INSTRUMENTS [M]

**PMMC (Permanent Magnet Moving Coil) — D'Arsonval movement:**
- DC only (or with rectifier for AC)
- Linear scale, accurate, low power consumption
- High torque/weight ratio
- Sensitivity: µA to mA range typically

**Moving Iron:**
- Both AC and DC (less accurate on DC due to hysteresis)
- Non-linear scale (compressed at low end)
- Cheaper than PMMC
- Used in panel voltmeters, ammeters

**Dynamometer:**
- Both AC and DC, accurate
- Used as wattmeter (current coil + voltage coil)
- More expensive

**Induction type:**
- AC only
- Used in AC energy meters (kWh meters at home)
- Disc rotates proportional to power

**Rectifier type:**
- PMMC + bridge rectifier
- Measures AC (RMS-calibrated)
- Good frequency response, lower cost

**Electrostatic:**
- For high voltages (kV range)
- Very high impedance, no power consumption
- Used in HV labs

**Errors:**
- **Systematic (instrumental):** can be calibrated
- **Random:** environmental, drift
- **Insertion error:** instrument loading

**CT and PT (Current and Potential Transformers) [M]:**

**CT (Current Transformer):**
- Primary in series with line; secondary shorted (NEVER open)
- Ratio: $I_p/I_s = N_s/N_p$
- Burden: secondary impedance + leads
- Accuracy class: e.g., 0.5, 1.0, 5P10 (protection)
- **NEVER OPEN THE SECONDARY** — generates lethal high voltage (kV) and saturation

**PT (Potential Transformer):**
- Primary across line; secondary to voltmeter
- Ratio: $V_p/V_s = N_p/N_s$
- Used in HV measurement (kV → 110V for metering)

**🚩 EXAMINER TRAP:** "Why must CT secondary be shorted?" → If open, primary current becomes magnetizing current → core saturates → very high secondary voltage → insulation breakdown + lethal. Same reason you never run a transformer with secondary open at high ratios.

### 2.7 MEASUREMENT OF POWER, ENERGY, POWER FACTOR [M]

**Wattmeters:** Electrodynamometer (AC + DC), Induction (AC only)

**Two-wattmeter method (3-φ, 3-wire):**
$$P = W_1 + W_2$$
$$\tan\phi = \sqrt{3}(W_1 - W_2)/(W_1 + W_2)$$
- Used when neutral is not available

**Three-wattmeter method (3-φ, 4-wire):**
- Each phase has its own wattmeter
- More accurate for unbalanced loads

**Energy meters (kWh):**
- Induction type, with braking magnet
- Disc rotates proportional to power
- Alumel/U-type magnets for temperature compensation
- Modern: electronic (static) energy meters

**Power factor meters:**
- Single-phase: based on phase angle
- 3-phase: based on cross-phase relationships

### 2.8 OSCILLOSCOPE [M]

**CRO (Cathode Ray Oscilloscope):**
- Displays voltage waveform vs time
- Controls:
  - Time/div: horizontal scale
  - Volts/div: vertical scale
  - Trigger: synchronizes sweep with input
  - AC/DC coupling: AC removes DC, DC includes

**Measurements:**
- **Voltage:** peak-to-peak distance × V/div
- **Period:** distance between two same-phase points × time/div
- **Frequency:** $f = 1/period$
- **Phase:** measure time difference between two waveforms

**Lissajous figures:** For phase and frequency measurement
- Phase = $\sin^{-1}(y_1/y_2)$ for some patterns
- Frequency ratio: count horizontal/vertical loops

**Real-life anchor:** Every electronics lab in India uses CROs. Now being replaced by digital oscilloscopes (DSOs) with USB connectivity, FFT, etc.

### 2.9 DIGITAL ELECTRONICS BASICS [M]

**Logic gates:**
- AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR, XOR, XNOR
- NAND and NOR are **universal** (can implement any other gate)

**Boolean identities:**
- $A + \overline{A} = 1$, $A \cdot \overline{A} = 0$
- $A + AB = A$, $A(A + B) = A$
- $A + \overline{A}B = A + B$
- $\overline{A \cdot B} = \overline{A} + \overline{B}$ (De Morgan)
- $\overline{A + B} = \overline{A} \cdot \overline{B}$ (De Morgan)

**Flip-flops:**
- SR (Set-Reset): basic, has invalid state
- D (Data/Delay): one input, simple
- JK: like SR but with toggle
- T (Toggle): toggles on each clock

**Counters:**
- Mod-N counter uses N flip-flops
- Ripple counter: asynchronous, slower
- Synchronous counter: faster, all FFs clocked together

**ADC and DAC:**
- **ADC types:** Flash (fastest), Successive Approximation (SAR, common), Dual slope (accurate)
- **DAC types:** R-2R ladder (most common), Weighted resistor, Thermometer
- Resolution = $V_{ref}/2^n$ (n bits)

**Multiplexer:** $2^n$ inputs, n select lines, 1 output
- Used in data routing, function generation
- Demultiplexer: opposite (1 input, $2^n$ outputs)

**Schmitt trigger:** Comparator with hysteresis
- Used in: noise rejection, square wave generation, signal conditioning

### 2.10 ANALOG ELECTRONICS [M]

**Diode:**
- $I = I_s (e^{V/V_T} - 1)$ (Shockley equation)
- $V_T = kT/q \approx 26$ mV at 300 K
- Forward drop: 0.7V (Si), 0.3V (Ge)
- Reverse breakdown: Zener (intentional, used as voltage reference)

**BJT (Bipolar Junction Transistor):**
- NPN and PNP
- $I_C = \beta I_B$, $I_E = (\beta + 1) I_B$
- $\alpha = \beta / (\beta + 1)$ (common-base current gain)
- Active region: BE forward-biased, BC reverse-biased

**FET (Field Effect Transistor):**
- JFET, MOSFET
- $I_D = I_{DSS}(1 - V_{GS}/V_P)^2$ (JFET, square law)
- MOSFET: enhancement or depletion mode

**Biasing:**
- Need to set Q-point (DC operating point)
- Common configurations: fixed bias, voltage divider bias (most stable), emitter bias, collector feedback bias

**Amplifier configurations (BJT):**
| Config | $A_v$ | $A_i$ | $R_{in}$ | $R_{out}$ | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CE | High | High | Medium | Medium | General purpose |
| CB | High | Low | Low | High | HF, impedance matching |
| CC (emitter follower) | ~1 | High | High | Low | Buffer |

**Op-Amp (operational amplifier) [M]:**
- Ideal: $A_v = \infty$, $R_{in} = \infty$, $R_{out} = 0$, infinite CMRR, infinite bandwidth
- Two rules: (1) no current into inputs, (2) $V_+ = V_-$ (virtual short)

**Common Op-Amp circuits:**
- **Inverting amplifier:** $V_{out} = -(R_f/R_{in}) V_{in}$
- **Non-inverting amplifier:** $V_{out} = (1 + R_f/R_{in}) V_{in}$
- **Differential amplifier:** $V_{out} = (R_f/R_{in})(V_+ - V_-)$
- **Integrator:** $V_{out} = -(1/RC) \int V_{in} dt$
- **Differentiator:** $V_{out} = -RC \cdot dV_{in}/dt$
- **Comparator:** output saturates + or - based on input sign
- **Summing amplifier:** $V_{out} = -R_f(V_1/R_1 + V_2/R_2 + ...)$

**Real-life anchor:** Every audio system, instrumentation amplifier, and analog signal processing circuit uses op-amps. The 741 is the classic op-amp IC.

### 2.11 8085 MICROPROCESSOR BASICS [L — 1 Q if any]

**Key facts:**
- 8-bit microprocessor
- 16-bit address bus → 64 KB addressable memory
- 8-bit data bus
- 40 pins, +5V single supply
- 74 instructions
- 5 interrupt pins: TRAP, RST 7.5, RST 6.5, RST 5.5, INTR
- 7 general-purpose registers: A, B, C, D, E, H, L (8-bit each)
- Register pairs: BC, DE, HL (16-bit)
- Special: SP (Stack Pointer), PC (Program Counter)

**Interrupt priority (highest to lowest):**
TRAP > RST 7.5 > RST 6.5 > RST 5.5 > INTR

**TRAP:**
- Non-maskable (cannot be disabled)
- Edge + level triggered
- Highest priority
- Vectored to 0024H

**INTR:**
- Maskable (can be enabled/disabled)
- Level triggered
- Lowest priority
- Non-vectored (external hardware provides vector)

**Address bus: 16 bits (A0-A15)**
**Data bus: 8 bits (AD0-AD7) — multiplexed with lower 8 bits of address**

**Addressing modes (basic):**
- Direct: LDA 2000H (3 bytes)
- Register: MOV A, B (1 byte)
- Immediate: MVI A, 05H (2 bytes)
- Indirect: MOV A, M (uses HL as address pointer)

**Common instructions:**
- MOV A, B (copy B to A)
- MVI A, 05H (load 05H into A immediately)
- ADD B (A = A + B)
- SUB B (A = A - B)
- ANA B (A = A AND B)
- ORA B (A = A OR B)
- CMA (complement A)
- INR A (increment A)
- DCR A (decrement A)
- JMP 2000H (unconditional jump)
- JZ 2000H (jump if zero)
- CALL 2000H (call subroutine)
- RET (return from subroutine)
- HLT (halt)

**🚩 EXAMINER TRAP:** "How many address lines?" 16. "How much memory?" 64 KB = 2^16. "How many data lines?" 8. "Operating voltage?" +5V.

**Real-life anchor:** 8085 was the workhorse of Indian engineering education in the 80s-90s. Now superseded by ARM, RISC-V, but still in syllabus for understanding microprocessor fundamentals.

## 7. SAMPLE INTERACTION

> **Student:** "Teach me tractive effort"
>
> **Tutor response:**
>
> ### Tractive Effort
>
> **STEP 1 — First principles:** Power = Force × Velocity. So Force = Power / Velocity. At the wheel-rail contact, the locomotive applies a tangential force (tractive effort) and moves at velocity $v$. The mechanical power delivered = $F \times v$. Electrical power input = $F \times v / \eta$ (where $\eta$ = motor + gear efficiency).
>
> **STEP 2 — Real-life anchor:** Every Indian Railways locomotive must overcome (a) train weight × gradient, (b) rolling friction, (c) air drag. The total required tractive effort at the wheel must be ≤ the weight on driven wheels × adhesion coefficient. Otherwise, the wheels slip (like a car on ice). A WAP-5 locomotive with 6000 hp (4.5 MW) at 100 km/h can produce $F = 375 \times 6000/100 = 22,500$ N — enough to pull a 1500-tonne train.
>
> **STEP 3 — Exam form:**
> $$F = \frac{375 \times \eta \times P_{hp}}{V_{km/h}} \text{ N}$$
> $$F = \frac{1000 \times \eta \times P_{kW}}{v_{m/s}} \text{ N}$$
> - $P_{hp}$ = power at wheels (or motor shaft, depending on convention) in horsepower
> - $P_{kW}$ = same in kilowatts
> - $V_{km/h}$ = velocity in km/h; $v_{m/s}$ = velocity in m/s
> - $\eta$ = efficiency (typically 0.85-0.92)
>
> **STEP 4 — Examiner tweak:**
> - *Tweak A:* "Find tractive effort for 4000 hp locomotive at 80 km/h" → $F = 375 \times 4000/80 = 18,750$ N
> - *Tweak B:* "Express in kgf" → divide N by 9.81 → 1 kgf = 9.81 N
> - *Tweak C:* "Power at motor shaft vs at wheels" → at wheels = at motor × η_gear × η_motor
> - *Tweak D:* "Find max velocity" given F = some value and motor power → $v = P/F$
>
> **STEP 5 — Practice:**
>
> *Q1 (Easy):* A locomotive has 5000 hp at the wheels and runs at 90 km/h. Find tractive effort.
> *Solution:* $F = 375 \times 5000/90 = 20,833$ N ≈ 2123 kgf.
>
> *Q2 (Medium):* A 3000 hp locomotive pulls a 1000-tonne train. If efficiency = 0.85, what max grade can it climb at 50 km/h (neglecting friction)?
> *Solution:* $F = 375 \times 0.85 \times 3000/50 = 19,125$ N. Grade force = $1000 \times 1000 \times 9.81 \times \sin\theta \approx 9810 \sin\theta$. So $\sin\theta = 19,125/9810 = 1.95$ → wait, that's > 1, meaning the train can climb any grade (impossible in reality). Means train can accelerate instead. Let's set grade = 1% → $F_{grade} = 1000 \times 1000 \times 9.81 \times 0.01 = 98,100$ N. Surplus = 19,125 - 98,100 = negative → locomotive can't climb 1% grade at 50 km/h. **This shows the F-v tradeoff in traction design.**
>
> *Q3 (Tricky — combined subject):* A 3-φ induction motor locomotive draws 4000 kVA at 0.85 pf from 25 kV catenary. What is its tractive effort at 60 km/h if motor + gear efficiency = 0.9?
> *Solution:* $P_{in} = 4000 \times 0.85 = 3400$ kW. $P_{wheels} = 3400 \times 0.9 = 3060$ kW = 4102 hp. $F = 375 \times 4102/60 = 25,638$ N.
>
> **CROSS-SUBJECT:** The tractive effort formula $F = P/v$ is the same as the power equation $P = T \cdot \omega$ (with $F$ replacing torque and $v$ replacing $\omega r$). Same physics: power = force × velocity (in linear) = torque × angular velocity (in rotational). The "wheel-rail interface" is where this conversion happens.
>
> **REVISION CARD:** $F(N) = 375 \times P_{hp} \times \eta / V_{km/h}$. Indian Railways: 25 kV AC, 50 Hz, 3-φ induction motor with VFD. 1 kgf = 9.81 N. Adhesion limits: $\mu = 0.25-0.35$ for steel wheel on steel rail. SEC: 50-80 Wh/tonne-km for electric, 250-400 for diesel.
